House prices in real terms

Headline house prices are nominal - they are the cash figures, and they take no account of money itself losing value along the way. A price that has doubled has not doubled in worth if everything else has doubled alongside it. Real prices strip that out, so that a pound in 1995 and a pound today stand for the same amount of buying power, and across much of the UK the gap between the two measures is the whole story.

This map shows the change in average house prices for every local authority in England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, drawn from the UK House Price Index. Choose any two dates, pick a property type, and switch between real and nominal to see how much of a rise was genuine and how much of it was only inflation.

Common questions

What are real house prices?
Real house prices are prices adjusted for inflation, so that figures from different years can be compared directly. Nominal, or cash, prices are the unadjusted numbers quoted in the news: they rise partly because houses have become more valuable, and partly because money has become less so.
Have UK house prices fallen in real terms?
Across much of the country, yes, over any period reaching back to the 2007 peak - even where the cash price has risen over the same years. Set the date range on the map and switch the price basis to real to see the answer for a particular area.
How are these prices adjusted for inflation?
Every monthly figure is deflated by the ONS Consumer Prices Index and expressed in the money of the most recent month in the data, so any two points on the chart are directly comparable.
Where does the data come from?
The UK House Price Index, produced by HM Land Registry, the ONS, Registers of Scotland and Land & Property Services Northern Ireland, which is published monthly. The inflation series is ONS Consumer Prices Index, all items.
